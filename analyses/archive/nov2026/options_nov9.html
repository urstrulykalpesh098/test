Read this first. There is no hard evidence here, and I won't pretend otherwise.
  • No real option prices were available. Alpha Vantage's option chains are a paid endpoint, FMP has none, and the Cboe chains in your readiness report only work through your Mac's browser. Every price below is a model estimate (Black-Scholes, anchored to VIX 25.64, with an assumed put skew of 0.9 vol points per 1% out of the money).
  • "Edge" means my simulation disagrees with my option model. Both rest on my assumptions. That is a hypothesis to test, not proof the market is mispriced.
  • 1:2 is not the same as good. A 1:2 trade that wins 25% of the time loses money. The column that matters is expected value per $1 risked, and whether it survives options costing 25% more than my model says.
  • Each trade has a hard check you can make on Monday: the most you can pay and still have 1:2. If the live price is above it, the trade fails your own rule. Skip it.

Why this one. It is the only trade you can place on Monday without waiting for news that still has positive expected value when options cost 25% more than my model (+0.03 per $1 risked; +0.28 at model prices). The option model gives SPY a 41% chance of finishing below the long strike, against 47% in the simulation. That gap comes from the mechanics in your reports: the rising 200-day, trend followers flipping, and vol-target selling.

How thin it is. The estimated cost of $3.04 is only 9% under the $3.33 ceiling. If real put skew is steeper than I assumed, the live price will be over the line and the trade is off.

Handling it. Close it by 3:45pm Friday. Don't hold through expiry, because SPY settling between the strikes risks assignment on the short put. This test only covered holding to Friday; early profit-taking wasn't tested. The worst case is losing the full $1,822, which happens in 53% of simulated weeks.

What the ten taught

  • Upside bets lose in this simulation. Call spreads and the call butterfly have negative expected value, because the simulated week leans lower and has a fatter downside tail. The options price that downside tail too, so buying calls is not where the gap is.
  • Waiting for confirmation costs more than it earns. Buying puts after a confirmed 200-day break (T2) is worse than buying before it (T1). By Monday's close, the move and the higher volatility are already in the price.
  • Butterflies are cheap but rarely pay in full. 1:9 reward to risk sounds great, but SPY has to finish close to one exact strike. They almost never pay the maximum.
  • The one real gap is the 200-day mechanics, and it is small. It shows up as a few points of probability, which a steeper real skew could erase.

Monday checklist

  • Get the live bid and ask for both legs of the spread. Use the mid as your limit, and never pay more than the "pay no more than" figure.
  • If the spread between bid and ask on either leg is wider than about $0.10, the slippage assumption fails. Recheck the numbers.
  • Compare the real implied vol of the 730 put with the model's (about 26% here). If it is much higher, puts are pricier than modelled and the gap is smaller.
  • Don't enter in the first 15 minutes. Spreads are widest right after the open.
  • Size so the full loss is money you can lose. Defined-risk spreads can and often do expire worthless.

Not tested, and why

  • Single stocks (CoreWeave, Nvidia, the chip ETFs): there's no option data for them here, and the simulation only models the index. Your reports say CoreWeave's options price about a 9-point move and Nvidia's about ±8.5%, with Nvidia beating its implied move only ~29% of the time. Selling that premium needs more margin than $2,000 allows.
  • Expiries after 13 Nov: valuing them on Friday would need an assumption about future volatility on top of everything else.
  • Early exits and stop-losses: every trade here is held to Friday.

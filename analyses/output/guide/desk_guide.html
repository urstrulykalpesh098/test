One call, start to finish

Real call mb27-up, made 26 Nov

The question: will the S&P 500 close Friday 27 Nov above Wednesday's 7,182.78? Three separate forecasters answer it, the desk's own answer gets corrected for its track record, and the three are averaged into one number. Friday's close grades it.

  1. Desk judgment, 52%. Claude reads the tape: futures were up 0.6% early (counted at half weight, lesson L1), Europe rallied, but the S&P sat below its 20, 50 and 200-day averages and three opening gaps in a row had failed.
  2. Calibrated, 37%. When the desk said 50 to 70% in the past, it came true only 30% of the time (20 calls). The rule pulls 52% toward that record: (20 × 0.30 + 10 × 0.52) ÷ 30 = 0.373.
  3. VIX formula, 50%. The no-opinion baseline. It assumes no drift and a daily move sized by the VIX. For "above today's price" that is exactly 50% by construction.
  1. Simulator, 46.7%. A script replays the last 60 daily moves in random order 200,000 times, scaled to today's volatility, and counts how many paths end up.
  2. Final, 45%. Equal thirds until each forecaster has 20 graded calls: (0.373 + 0.50 + 0.467) ÷ 3 = 0.447. After that, the forecaster with the better recent score gets more weight.
  3. Graded at Friday's close. If the S&P closes up, this call scores (1 − 0.447)² = 0.31. If down, (0 − 0.447)² = 0.20. The call also stores what would make it wrong: "an AI or tariff headline in the thin half session" (tag: gap reversal).
Check yourself: if the calibration bin had said 50 to 70% calls came true 60% of the time instead of 30%, what would the calibrated number be?

(20 × 0.60 + 10 × 0.52) ÷ 30 = 0.573. The rule only drags the number down when the desk has been overconfident in that bin.

How a call gets graded

The Brier score

Each call is a probability that a claim comes true. When it resolves, the claim gets 1 (happened) or 0 (didn't), and the score is (probability − outcome)². Zero is perfect, 0.25 is what you get by always saying 50%, and 1 is confidently wrong. In the ledger, hit means the claim happened and miss means it didn't. That is not the same as the desk being right: a 25% call on something that doesn't happen is a good call.

Check yourself: the desk says 70% and the claim fails. What's the Brier score, and is that better or worse than a coin flip?

(0.70 − 0)² = 0.49, almost twice as bad as always saying 50%. That is why overconfidence hurts more than timidity.

Trackers: context, never a vote

All from free primary sources

Each tracker runs in the close scorecard from a script copy stored in the ledger. They can name a warning in the day's watch list, but they never move a probability until the Sunday review has tested them on at least 20 days.

Flows and positioning

Who is holding what. CFTC futures positioning (dealers, asset managers, hedge funds), FINRA margin debt (money borrowed to buy stocks), SPY and IVV shares outstanding (new shares mean money flowing in), and leveraged ETF rebalancing.

Example of the rebalance maths. A 3x fund must trade L(L−1) × the day's move × its assets near the close. A 3x fund holding $10B on a −2% day sells about 3 × 2 × 2% × $10B = $1.2B. Illustration, not a real fund.

Credit and funding

The bond market's mood. High-yield and investment-grade spreads (extra yield over Treasuries, in basis points, 1bp = 0.01%), the Chicago Fed's financial conditions index, SOFR against the Fed's IORB rate (repo cash costing more than the Fed pays banks means funding is tight), and SEC filings for new bond deals.

The divergence alert fires when high-yield spreads widen 40bp in 20 days while the S&P is within 2% of its 60-day high: bonds worrying while stocks don't. Only 2 past episodes since Nov 2023, so it is a watch item.

Treasury auctions

Demand for government debt. Bid-to-cover is bids divided by the amount sold (higher means more demand); dealer share is what the big banks had to absorb because others didn't bid (higher means weaker). Each auction is compared with the last six of the same maturity.

Policy and permitting

Every Texas bill filed for the 2027 session, tagged by market theme, plus new federal rules from energy, environment and market regulators. A filed bill is not a passed bill.

Event engine

Earnings for 16 watchlist names (NVDA, AMD, ORCL and others) and 11 macro releases (CPI, payrolls, FOMC). Two calls per event: does the move beat what options imply, and does it close up? Example: options implied an 8.3% Nvidia move; it moved 2.9%.

Real option chains

Real SPY quotes from MarketData.app's free plan (bid, ask, implied volatility, open interest), one session delayed. They price the paper book. One request per run to save credits; the trial is used freely and every credit is logged.

Ranges that hold 80% of the time

Rule 5, version 5, approved 26 Nov

An 80% range is a claim that the close lands inside it four days out of five. Too narrow and it breaks constantly; too wide and it says nothing. The old rule (1.6 times the VIX-implied move) held 98% of days since 2022: safe but useless.

The new rule sizes the range from how much the S&P actually moved over the last 20 days (realized volatility), times 0.96. In a storm (VIX above 25, or the 3-month VIX below the 1-month, called an inverted curve), it never goes narrower than the VIX implies. Then: range = last close × e±1.28 × σ × √days, where 1.28 is the z-score that leaves 10% in each tail.

Worked example, from the nightly build. On 24 Nov the S&P closed 7,201.09 with VIX above 25 (a storm). Daily σ came to about 1.93%, and 1.28 × 1.93% = 2.47%, so the next-day range was 7,028 to 7,379. The VIX band, built the same way from the VIX's own daily changes, was 20.9 to 33.2.

Held = share of days the close landed inside. Tested on 2022 to 2026 data the rule never saw. November's shock still breaks it: in the last 15 days the new S&P range held only 53%, so when the prior day moved more than twice what VIX priced, the desk says ranges may run narrow.

Check yourself: why is the multiplier 1.28 and not 1.96?

1.96 leaves 2.5% in each tail, which is a 95% range. An 80% range leaves 10% in each tail, and the z-score for that is 1.28.

Calibration: does 70% mean 70%?

From the 26 Nov check, 111 calls

A forecaster is calibrated when its 70% calls come true about 70% of the time. The desk's 70 to 85% calls came true only 55%, and 25 of those 32 calls were ranges. The ranges were the leak, which is why rule 5 changed.

Rule 2 fixes the rest: each call's raw number is pulled toward how often that confidence bin has come true, weighted by how many calls are in the bin (worked in the mb27-up example above). Fancier fixes were tested walk-forward and did no better.

One rule was dropped. The desk seemed to score 0.175 on quiet days and 0.31 on trend days, but that labelled each call using the day it resolved, which nobody knows in advance. Labelled with what was known when the call was made, the split flipped, so the trend-day rule is suspended.

Rule 2, added 26 Nov after the miss autopsy

The Sunday miss autopsy groups graded calls by possible cause and checks whether each group's bad score could be luck, by shuffling the labels thousands of times. Two groups stood out.

The shrink lifts by itself once a group scores at or below the desk's overall Brier on 10 new graded calls. The Sunday review checks, and a reminder is set for 10 Dec.

The lab: testing beliefs on ten years

On HQ, rebuilt nightly

Self-research loop

Every desk belief is checked on data before 2022 (where ideas get fitted) and then on 2022 onwards (data it never saw). Only beliefs that pass the second test become proposals, and nothing changes without your yes. The 1.6x range rule failed here, which led to rule 5 v5.

Seven specialist analysts

Trend, bounce, fear gauge, vol curve, credit, rates and dollar, each watching one signal and refit every quarter on past data only. On about 1,950 unseen days, none beat the plain "the S&P rises 54% of days" rate by more than noise. The lesson: don't expect skill on daily direction. These are being archived (see below).

Playbooks: days like today

The 150 past days most like the latest close (24 Nov: VIX 26.4, S&P down 1.9% in 5 days and 6.8% in 20, inverted VIX curve). The S&P was higher the next day 80% of the time, against 55% on all days. Tested honestly, analogs don't beat the up-rate, so read it as the spread of outcomes, not a forecast.

Ten-market board

S&P, Nasdaq, Dow, VIX, 10-year and 2-year yields, the high-yield spread, WTI oil, the broad dollar and CAD per USD, each with its own range and up chance, graded automatically by the nightly job. You can lock in your own guesses on HQ before seeing the desk's.

The paper book

A test of the calls, never advice

A virtual $10,000 that trades SPY option spreads only when the desk and the plain formula disagree by 7 points or more on an S&P level. The idea: if the desk has an edge, money follows the disagreements.

Defined risk only: a vertical spread buys one option and sells another nearby, so the most it can lose is known up front. At most 2% of the book at risk per trade, one new trade a day, three open at most. Take profit at 80% of the maximum, otherwise hold to expiry.

Prices are real quotes from the prior session, filled at mid plus half the bid/ask spread on every leg, which is deliberately unkind. The equity curve is compared with simply holding SPY from the same day.

Glossary

The jargon on these pages

VIX
The market's price for S&P volatility over the next 30 days, as an annual %. VIX 24 implies a typical daily move of about 24 ÷ √252 ≈ 1.5%.
Realized volatility
How much prices actually moved, measured from recent daily returns. In November it ran 31.7% against an average VIX of 23.9.
σ (sigma)
Standard deviation: the size of a typical move. A one-day range scales with σ; a five-day range with σ × √5.
Brier score
(probability − outcome)², averaged. 0 is perfect, 0.25 is a coin flip.
Calibration
Whether calls at a given confidence come true that often.
Out of sample
Testing a rule on data that wasn't used to build it. The only test that counts.
Implied move
The move options are priced for, usually from a straddle (a call plus a put at the same strike).
Spread (credit)
Extra yield a company pays over a Treasury of the same maturity. Wider means lenders are more nervous.
Basis point (bp)
0.01%. A spread going from 3.00% to 3.40% widened 40bp.
Bid-to-cover
Bids received ÷ bonds sold at an auction. Higher means stronger demand.
Regime
The day's label: Chop (moved under 0.5%), Risk-off, Relief rally, Slide or Grind up. For the record only.
Pre-mortem
Before a call resolves, writing the single most likely thing that would make it wrong.
Vertical spread
Buy one option, sell another of the same type and expiry at a different strike. Caps both loss and gain.
FRED
The St. Louis Fed's free database of official economic and market series. The desk's backbone.
